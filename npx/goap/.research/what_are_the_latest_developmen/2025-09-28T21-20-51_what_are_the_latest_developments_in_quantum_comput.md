# Search Results

**Query:** What are the latest developments in quantum computing?
**Date:** 2025-09-28T21:20:51.398Z

## Answer

Quantum computing has entered a phase of rapid advancement, with 2024–2025 marked by breakthroughs in **hardware scalability, error correction, quantum networking, and the integration of AI**. The field is transitioning from experimental demonstrations to early commercial and practical deployments, with significant implications for computing, cryptography, and industry.

**Key Developments in Quantum Computing (2024–2025):**

- **Logical Qubits and Error Correction:**  
  The focus has shifted from increasing the number of physical qubits to creating and stabilizing **logical qubits**—combinations of physical qubits that encode information robustly against errors. Major players like Google, IBM, Microsoft, and startups such as Alice & Bob and Riverlane have demonstrated that increasing the number of physical qubits used for error correction can lower logical error rates below critical thresholds, a prerequisite for practical, fault-tolerant quantum computing[9][5]. Notably, Google's Willow chip and Microsoft's Majorana 1 processor have set new benchmarks for error correction and logical qubit stability[5][9].

- **Topological and Photonic Qubits:**  
  **Topological qubits**, pursued by Microsoft, promise inherent error resistance due to their exotic quantum states, potentially enabling room-temperature operation and much longer coherence times[2][5]. **Photonic quantum computing**, championed by PsiQuantum, leverages photons' natural resistance to decoherence and compatibility with existing fiber-optic infrastructure, aiming for million-qubit, utility-scale machines[2][5].

- **Magic State Distillation and Universal Computation:**  
  A long-standing barrier—creating high-fidelity "magic states" for universal quantum computation—has been overcome. Recent experiments have demonstrated practical magic state distillation in logical qubits, enabling quantum computers to run non-Clifford gates and thus execute algorithms that cannot be simulated classically[3].

- **Quantum Networking and Distributed Systems:**  
  Advances in **quantum networking** include the successful entanglement of qubits across separate quantum computers and even between cities, laying the groundwork for distributed quantum computing and the future quantum internet[9][6]. Modular quantum architectures, where quantum processors can be linked and upgraded like building blocks, are also emerging[1][5].

- **AI–Quantum Synergy:**  
  The intersection of **AI and quantum computing** is accelerating progress in both fields. AI is being used to optimize quantum circuits, design new error correction codes, and even discover novel quantum algorithms. Conversely, quantum computers are beginning to tackle AI training tasks that are intractable for classical machines, creating a feedback loop that could drive exponential advances[1][2][4].

- **Commercialization and Cloud Access:**  
  Quantum cloud services from IBM, Google, Amazon, and Microsoft are making quantum hardware accessible to researchers and businesses, enabling experimentation and early application development without the need for in-house quantum hardware[5].

- **Quantum Sensing and Communication:**  
  Beyond computing, **quantum sensing** and **quantum communication** are reaching deployment. Quantum sensors are being used in defense, navigation, and semiconductor analysis, while quantum key distribution (QKD) and secure quantum networks are being piloted for cybersecurity[4][1].

- **Industry and Government Investment:**  
  Public and private investment in quantum technology is surging, with governments worldwide committing billions to quantum research, infrastructure, and workforce development. The market for quantum technologies is projected to reach tens of billions of dollars by 2035, with quantum computing capturing the largest share[4][2].

**Challenges and Outlook:**

- **Scalability:**  
  Building quantum computers with millions of reliable qubits remains a major engineering challenge, though advances in error correction and new qubit types are accelerating progress[2].

- **Hardware Diversity:**  
  Multiple hardware platforms (superconducting, trapped ions, photonic, topological, neutral atoms) are being pursued in parallel, each with unique strengths and challenges[5][9].

- **Security and Cryptography:**  
  The threat quantum computers pose to current cryptographic systems is driving urgent research into post-quantum cryptography, even as quantum communication offers new paradigms for secure data transfer[4].

- **Practical Applications:**  
  Early applications are emerging in quantum chemistry, optimization, and machine learning, with industries such as finance, pharmaceuticals, and logistics expected to be among the first to benefit[5].

**Representative Breakthroughs (2024–2025):**

- **Google’s Willow chip**: Demonstrated exponential speedup and below-threshold error correction[5][9].
- **Microsoft’s Majorana 1**: First topological qubit processor, designed for scalability and stability[5].
- **PsiQuantum’s photonic approach**: Announced plans for a utility-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computer using photonic qubits[2][5].
- **Magic state distillation**: Achieved in logical qubits, enabling universal quantum computation[3].
- **Quantum networking**: Entanglement and communication between distant quantum processors demonstrated[9][6].
- **AI–quantum integration**: AI-driven quantum circuit optimization and quantum-enhanced AI model training[1][2][4].

Quantum computing is now moving from laboratory proof-of-concept to early-stage commercial and practical deployment, with the next few years expected to bring further breakthroughs in scalability, error correction, and real-world applications[4][5].

##### Quantum computing’s six most important trends for 2025

The financial industry is anticipated to become one of the earliest adopters of commercially useful quantum computing technologies. These technologies are expected to become available within the next few years, making it more important than ever to follow experimental developments. Therefore, as 2024 comes to a close, we pause to reflect upon what has recently transpired and look for trends we believe will continue through 2025 and beyond. This exercise has led to the identification of six trends, each of which we cover through 2024 reports and announcements:

- More experiments with logical qubits

- More specialized hardware/software (as opposed to universal quantum computing)

- More networking noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices together

- More layers of software abstraction

- More workforce development tools

- Improved and novel physical qubits

These trends are not ordered by significance or likelihood; we see all of these as equally likely.... ##### More experiments with logical qubits

In “The road to useful quantum computing,” published on February 13, 2024, we reviewed the events of 2023 that started shifting our conversations from physical qubits in 2022 to logical qubits in 2024. Whereas a “physical qubit” refers to actual error-prone hardware, a “logical qubit” is an arrangement of these physical qubits that encodes information in such a way as to protect against errors [1]. We chronologically cited 19 achievements — many of which were experimental — from 23 organizations; alphabetically, they are: Alice & Bob, Amazon Web Services, Caltech, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Cornell, ETH Zurich, Fuzhou University, Google, Harvard, IBM, MIT, PsiQuantum, QC Design, Quantinuum, QuEra Computing, QuTech, Riverlane, Tsinghua University, the University of Chicago, the University of Science and Technology of China, the University of Sheffield, the University of Stuttgart, and Yale. We noted that by early 2024, QuEra and Infleqtion had already published their logical qubit roadmaps, while Alice & Bob had teased that a logical qubit roadmap would be forthcoming [2].... In 2024, we observed continued experimentation:

- On August 27, Google announced that it had demonstrated a quantum memory with below-threshold error rates and double the coherence lifetimes as compared with physical qubits [3].

- On September 10, Microsoft and Quantinuum announced that they had entangled 12 logical qubits, triple the logical qubit count from six months prior [4]. The physical error rate of 0.024 was brought down to a logical error rate of 0.0011, leading to the first ever chemistry simulation combining high-performance computing (HPC), artificial intelligence (AI), and quality control (QC) [5].

- On October 31, Rigetti and Riverlane announced that they had demonstrated error correction with gate speeds fast enough to support heterogeneous quantum-classical processing [6].

- On December 3, an IBM Quantum team announced that they had demonstrated the entanglement of logical qubits using overlapping codes, applying a method thought to be impractical with limited connectivity [7].

- On December 9, Google announced that its Willow chip demonstrated below-threshold error correction, lowering error rates as more physical qubits encode logical qubits [8].

We’ve also seen growth in the number of logical qubit roadmaps. In addition to the three companies we mentioned, Inside Quantum Technology mentioned IBM Quantum, Google, and Microsoft in “The quantum roadmap battle of logical qubits,” which was published on February 27, 2024 [9]. Since that time, we have added eight more to this list (alphabetically, given that not all of them are dated):

- Diraq [10]

- IonQ [11]

- IQM [12]

- Pasqal [13]

- PsiQuantum [14]

- Quantinuum [15]

- Quandela [16]

- Xanadu [17]

While this may not be an exhaustive list, it shows a continuing trend either way. We’ve gone from talking about physical qubits to demonstrating logical qubits to showing that logical qubits lower error rates. The growth in logical qubit roadmaps requires accelerated experimentation, especially considering that quantum computers based on logical qubits are being promised within the next few years.... ##### More specialized hardware/software (as opposed to universal QC)

Building a universal quantum computer — a quantum computer that can run any quantum algorithm — is hard [18]. If it were easy, we would already have one. While this is still the end goal, multiple companies are developing specialized quantum computers for specific problems to achieve earlier commercial value:

- Bleximo is building full-stack superconducting application-specific systems with co-designed processors, software, and control stacks [19].

- Qilimanjaro is building quantum app-specific integrated circuits (QASICs) for superconducting analog quantum computers with full stacks co-designed for specific problems [20].

- QuiX is building special-purpose photonic quantum computers for specific optimization and simulation problems [21].

- On November 19, QuEra launched a full-stack quantum algorithm co-design program to optimize hardware, software, and applications for specific problems [22].

It’s reasonable to assume that this list will continue to grow. Likewise, it’s presumably easier to sell a special-purpose quantum computer that provides a commercial advantage for one or a few problems than it is to sell a general-purpose quantum computer that does not yet provide commercial advantages for any problems.

##### More networking NISQ devices together

Two ways to scale up a quantum computer are to add more qubits to a single quantum computer or to interconnect multiple quantum computers, creating a single virtual quantum computer with a higher qubit count. In 2024, several companies announced larger individual processors, but there were also developments in connecting multiple processors:

- On May 30, Photonic announced that it had demonstrated distributed entanglement, linking qubits within separate quantum computers [23].

- On October 30, QuTech announced that it had connected two small quantum computers in two different cities [24].

- IBM is building L-couplers to scale its systems [25]. On November 21, it announced that it had classically linked two 127-qubit quantum processors to create a virtual 142-qubit system [26].

There is far more research going into quantum networks than the examples here, but not all of it links to quantum computers just yet. Researchers are still developing the infrastructure. However, experimental demonstrations of interconnected and internetworked quantum computers are bound to lead to experimentation with distributed applications using more of these nascent quantum networks.... ##### Improved and novel physical qubits

Two ways to lower error rates on quantum computers are to improve the physical qubits and to implement high-overhead error correction codes. It is widely believed that quantum error correction (QEC) will be necessary; however, research continues into improving physical qubits, which in turn boosts the performance of the logical qubits: [34]

- On May 6, the University of Basel announced the demonstration of hole spin qubits, which use the spins of missing electrons in semiconductors and require fewer components than electron spin qubits [35].

- On September 19, University College Dublin announced that "split-electrons," so-called “Majorana fermions,” can theoretically be used as topological qubits [36].

- On September 21, Brookhaven National Laboratory announced it had used constriction junctions, which simplify chip fabrication to enable mass production [37].

- On November 26, The Quantum Insider reported that Quantinuum, Harvard, and Caltech experimentally demonstrated the first “true topological qubit,” robustly encoding information in systems’ relationship patterns instead of within the systems [38].

- On November 26, as part of the Q-LEAP project, RIKEN and Toshiba announced a double-transmon coupler (DTC), which has been proposed to improve gate fidelity [39].

- On November 26, The Wall Street Journal reported that Ephos uses glass chips instead of silicon to reduce energy consumption and information loss [40].

- On December 4, the American Physical Society reported in its Physics publication that ETH Zurich demonstrated a mechanical qubit consisting of two sapphire chips, with a superconducting qubit on top with a mechanical resonator below [41].

It’s impossible to know everything that is being researched at any given time. We can assume, however, that research continues in universities and labs around the world, as well as at many quantum computing companies.... ##### Conclusion

While no one has a crystal ball, we can confidently predict 2025 trends based on analyses of 2024 and 2023. The exciting part of this is the unknowns. If we were in 2022, we probably wouldn’t be predicting the experimental demonstration of logical qubits in 2023, but that happened. We’re excited to find out what we’ve missed, and we hope you’ll join us throughout the year as we continue along the path toward fault-tolerant quantum computing.... ## Learn more

## Quantum computing has been at the forefront of technological advancements, holding the potential to revolutionise fields ranging from cryptography to materials science

Since 2023, the field has seen remarkable strides in hardware, algorithms, and practical applications.

This article provides an overview of the key developments shaping the quantum computing landscape.

### 1. Quantum hardware: Advancing the building blocks

One major breakthrough involves the development of hypercube network technologies, which enhance the scalability and performance of quantum systems. These networks are poised to overcome traditional limitations in communication between qubits, enabling more robust quantum computers. Khaleel, T.A. (2024).

Furthermore, integrated photonics has emerged as a promising avenue for scalable quantum computing with trapped ions. By combining advanced photonic components with ion traps, researchers are paving the way for more compact and efficient quantum devices. Mordini, C., & Mehta, K. (2024).

### 2. Post-quantum cryptography: Addressing emerging threats

The advent of quantum computers poses significant risks to classical cryptographic methods. Recent studies emphasize the importance of developing post-quantum cryptographic algorithms to secure sensitive data against quantum threats. These efforts are critical for safeguarding global communication networks. Dymova, H. (2024).... ### 3. Quantum machine learning integration

Machine learning is increasingly integrated with quantum systems to enhance computational power. For example, frameworks like TorchQC enable the application of deep learning techniques in quantum dynamics and control. This integration is expected to optimise quantum algorithms for real-world scenarios. Koutromanos, D., & Paspalakis, E. (2024).

### 4. Materials for quantum hardware

Innovative materials, such as germanium and GeSn-based quantum well lasers, are under development to improve quantum hardware. These materials promise higher efficiency and better performance for nanoscale quantum devices. Joshi, R.S. (2025).

### 5. Hybrid quantum-classical algorithms

Hybrid approaches, which leverage both quantum and classical systems, have gained traction. These methods aim to exploit the strengths of quantum systems while relying on classical systems for tasks that remain computationally challenging. Singh, P., & Raman, B. (2025).

### 6. Quantum optimisation techniques

Developments in quantum optimisation algorithms, such as those designed for Rydberg atom arrays, highlight the potential for solving complex problems in logistics, energy, and beyond. These algorithms demonstrate significant improvements over classical counterparts. Dlaska, C. (2024).

### 7. Quantum chemistry applications

Quantum computing’s application in quantum chemistry has enabled the computation of single-point energies of large molecular systems with unprecedented accuracy. This advancement could accelerate drug discovery and materials design. Barone, V. (2025).... ### 8. Practical implementations

Finally, real-world implementations of quantum computing have expanded. From advancing artificial intelligence to enhancing system technologies, the practical impact of quantum computing is becoming increasingly evident. Crilly, P.B. (2024).

Quantum computing is no longer confined to theoretical exploration; it is entering a phase of practical innovation and application. With advancements in hardware, algorithms, and integration with other technologies, quantum computing promises to reshape the technological landscape. The journey toward quantum supremacy may still have hurdles, but the progress made in recent years underscores its transformative potential.
